The Evolution of Solar Leases & PPAs

Understanding how California's solar financing market evolved from 2006 to today and why many homeowners are reconsidering assumptions formed years ago.

Many homeowners today are still evaluating solar leases and Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) using information they heard years ago.

That information may have been accurate at the time.

But California's solar market continues to evolve.

  • Utility rates changed.
  • Battery technology changed.
  • Consumer protections changed.
  • Federal tax treatment changed.
  • California's net-metering policies changed.

The purpose of this guide is to explain how we got here.

How the Conversation Evolved

Three Changes That Reshaped the Market

Why Leasing Opened The Market

Early third-party ownership dramatically reduced the upfront investment required to adopt solar. Instead of purchasing equipment outright, homeowners could access solar through long-term agreements while the provider owned and serviced the system.

Why Batteries Changed The Discussion

Solar produces during daylight hours. As battery technology became practical for residential adoption, homeowners gained the ability to store energy for later use rather than relying entirely on real-time production and utility interaction.

Why Agreements Require Actual Review

Roof coverage, transfers, production guarantees, monitoring, battery coverage, buyout rights, and end-of-term options vary by provider and contract. Understanding the actual agreement remains more important than assumptions.

Decision-Making in Practice

A California Homeowner Example

The homeowner was interested in:

  • Reducing utility exposure
  • Battery backup
  • Long-term planning
  • Energy-cost predictability

One California homeowner in PG&E territory was interested in going solar.

The homeowner had previously purchased solar on another property and initially assumed ownership would again be the obvious choice.

At the same time, utility costs continued increasing and future electricity usage was expected to grow.

Initially, the homeowner assumed ownership was automatically the best option.

However, after evaluating:

  • Utility-only costs
  • Cash purchase
  • ROI comparisons
  • Solar lease structures
  • Battery requirements
  • Remaining utility exposure
  • Upfront capital requirements
  • Future possible usage increases

the discussion became much broader.

The conversation was no longer simply: Should I buy solar?

Instead, the evaluation became: How should I manage energy costs and risk over the next 20+ years?

After comparing financial returns, risk exposure, peace of mind, flexibility and future energy needs, the homeowner ultimately concluded that a modern solar lease aligned most closely with the priorities that mattered most.

The important lesson was not that leasing is always better.
The lesson was that the evaluation became much bigger than ownership alone.

Common Assumptions

Important Ideas Worth Re-Examining

Select any topic to see why a complete evaluation matters.

Ownership may absolutely be the correct choice for some homeowners. However, ownership is only one variable. A complete evaluation may also include upfront capital, financing costs, battery requirements, remaining utility costs, service responsibilities, opportunity cost, and long-term cash flow.

Production and bill reduction are not identical calculations under current California structures. Solar production, battery operation, imports, exports and load timing all affect the result.

Consumer protections improved. Battery adoption expanded. Utility economics changed. Technology improved.

Modern batteries may also affect how and when solar energy is consumed, stored and purchased from the utility.

Monthly payment is only one factor. Total long-term cost, flexibility, maintenance responsibilities, energy goals and future changes all deserve consideration.

An Advisor's Point of View

Why My Perspective Changed

As someone who has worked in California residential solar since 2013, I spent many years in a market where ownership was commonly viewed as the preferred solution.

The environment today is different.

Utility costs are different
Battery economics are different
Consumer protections are different
Federal homeowner tax treatment is different

Many assumptions homeowners formed years ago deserve to be revisited using today's facts.

Modern PPAs and leases solve different problems.

For some homeowners they may provide:

  • Lower upfront investment
  • Battery access
  • Capital preservation
  • Defined service obligations
  • Simplified maintenance responsibilities
