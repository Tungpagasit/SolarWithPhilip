There isn't one meaningful price for “solar.”

Cost depends on electricity use, desired production, roof and property conditions, equipment, battery requirements, ownership structure, financing and the particular program being considered.

That's why comparing only the total price or monthly payment can be misleading.

Solar With Philip helps define what the system is intended to accomplish first, making the proposals that follow easier to compare.

A useful solar quote should show more than a monthly payment.

Compare:

  • system size
  • expected annual production
  • production assumptions
  • current and future expected electricity use
  • solar offset target
  • battery configuration
  • equipment
  • cash price or financial structure
  • financing, lease or PPA terms where applicable
  • warranties
  • installer responsibilities
  • expected remaining utility costs
  • important assumptions behind the projected economics

Solar produces electricity. Batteries change when stored energy can be used and may also provide backup capability depending on the system design.

The right combination depends on your electricity usage pattern, utility structure, solar production, evening consumption, backup goals, expected future usage and the economics of the available system.

Battery capacity should be part of the home-energy strategy rather than something added simply because batteries are available.

Start with electricity consumption rather than panel count.

Then consider what may change.

An EV, heat pump, electric water heater, additional HVAC usage, pool equipment or other electrification can materially change future electricity consumption.

From there, a production target can be developed.

A 125% or 150% energy-production target, for example, describes expected production relative to modeled consumption. It does not automatically mean the physical system or cost is 125% or 150% larger.

The proper target depends on the individual home. The Energy Strategies tool can help you think it through.
