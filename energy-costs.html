Energy Costs Page

The Future of Energy Costs in California

Looking Beyond Today’s Utility Bill

Most homeowners focus on this month’s bill.

The more important question may be:

What will electricity cost over the next 10, 20, or 25 years?

Energy costs affect far more than utility bills. They influence household budgets, retirement planning, business costs, home values, lifestyle choices, and financial flexibility.

This page is not about fear.

It is about understanding the forces that influence electricity prices and why many homeowners are beginning to pay closer attention.

A long-term view

Most Major Financial Decisions Are Made Before the Cost Becomes Obvious

People buy health insurance before they are sick.

People save for retirement before they stop working.

People purchase life insurance before they need it.

Energy may be no different.

By the time rising electricity costs become a serious financial burden, many of the easiest options may already be behind us.

The Goal Is Not to Predict the Future

The goal is to understand the direction. Nobody knows exactly what electricity will cost 10, 20, or 30 years from now. But history can provide clues, and informed people generally make better decisions than surprised people.

How this page was built

Before You Read Another Number, You Should Know Where the Information Comes From

One of the biggest challenges people face today is not a lack of information. It is an overwhelming amount of information coming from organizations that often have their own incentives, objectives, and viewpoints.

Sources intentionally not used as primary support

To reduce the influence of organizations with direct financial or political interests, this page does not use the following as primary support for its conclusions:

  • Utility-company marketing materials from PG&E, Southern California Edison, or San Diego Gas & Electric.
  • CPUC webpages or summaries as the principal interpretation of household cost.
  • Solar-company, installer, finance-provider, lease-provider, or lead-generation marketing materials.
  • Political talking points or fear-based predictions.

This does not mean every statement from these organizations is false. It means the organizations have roles and incentives that may influence what they emphasize. Original tariffs, rules, or filings may later be used only when necessary to verify a specific primary record, and they should be labeled clearly.

Sources preferred
  • Independent energy analysts with transparent methodology.
  • Public datasets that can be reviewed or reproduced.
  • Academic and economic research.
  • Independent consumer and affordability studies.
  • Multiple independent sources for important claims.
The Informed Homeowner Principle

Do not blindly trust utility companies. Do not blindly trust energy companies. Do not blindly trust politicians. Do not blindly trust websites, including this one.

Verify. Compare. Think critically. Ask what is being measured. Ask what is included and excluded. Ask whether a number is historical, averaged, discounted, modeled, preliminary, or projected. Follow the evidence wherever it leads.

What drives electricity costs?

The Reality Is More Complex Than One Company Simply Choosing a Price

Electricity pricing is influenced by many factors that continue to evolve. The sections below remain easy to scan while allowing readers to open the topics they want to understand.

Growing energy demand

As California becomes increasingly electrified, more activities that once relied on gasoline or natural gas are shifting to electricity.

  • Electric vehicles
  • Commercial fleets
  • Heat pumps and electric water heating
  • Data centers and artificial-intelligence infrastructure
  • Manufacturing and other large commercial loads

As demand grows or changes location and timing, additional generation, transmission, distribution, charging, storage, and management capacity may be required.

Aging infrastructure

California’s electric grid was built over decades. Infrastructure requires ongoing inspection, maintenance, upgrades, modernization, and replacement. These costs do not disappear simply because an individual household conserves electricity.

Wildfire prevention and vegetation management

Maintaining an electric system in wildfire-prone areas can include inspecting equipment, trimming trees, removing hazardous vegetation, hardening infrastructure, monitoring risk, rebuilding damaged facilities, and in selected situations undergrounding power lines.

These activities require labor, equipment, contractors, planning, environmental coordination, insurance, and financing. The finished page will link to independent reporting on these costs.

Renewable-energy transition and storage

Renewable generation can reduce fuel use, but a changing generation mix may also require transmission, storage, flexible demand, reserve capacity, and operating changes so electricity remains available when needed.

Reliability requirements

The electric system must continue operating during heat waves, extreme weather, wildfire conditions, equipment failures, and periods of high demand. Reliability requires planning and investment regardless of how much electricity one household uses.

Why one average can be misleading

Homeowners do not buy electricity through one universal rate. The actual cost may depend on rate plan, season, peak and off-peak periods, usage tier, fixed charges, billing days, and total consumption.

The final chart should therefore identify exactly what each line measures rather than presenting one analytical average as the price every household pays.

Electricity is becoming more important

A Generation Ago, Electricity Powered Lights and Appliances

Today electricity increasingly supports transportation, home comfort, work, internet connectivity, security, digital services, and technologies that are becoming embedded in daily life.

Transportation

Vehicle charging moves part of transportation energy from gasoline toward the electric system.

Homes

Cooling, heat pumps, water heating, batteries, home offices, and connected devices increase dependence on reliable electricity.

Business and Technology

Warehouses, fleet depots, manufacturing, data centers, communications, and artificial intelligence all require physical electrical infrastructure.

The demand picture

Homeowners Are Not the Only Ones Using More Electricity

The issue is not whether electrification is good or bad. The issue is understanding the scale, timing, location, and infrastructure required when more of the economy depends on electricity.

Residential and commercial charging are different

Residential charging is distributed across homes. Commercial charging can concentrate large power requirements at fleet yards, workplaces, warehouses, public charging sites, apartment properties, and transportation corridors.

Where and when charging occurs may matter as much as the total number of vehicles.

Why vehicle definitions matter

A battery-electric vehicle relies entirely on stored electricity. A plug-in hybrid uses electricity and fuel. A conventional hybrid typically does not charge from the grid. Combining the categories can misstate the amount of transportation demand reaching the electric system.

Potential drivers include EV charging, heat pumps, electric water heating, cooling, home offices, batteries, and new devices. Efficiency improvements may offset part of the growth.

Commercial fleets and logistics

Delivery fleets, buses, trucks, service vehicles, warehouses, and logistics centers may charge many vehicles at one location and require upgrades beyond the chargers themselves.

Data centers and artificial intelligence

Digital services require physical computing facilities, cooling, networking, and highly reliable power. Final charts must distinguish current electricity use from forecasts and clearly state whether data apply to California or a broader region.

Manufacturing and building electrification

Industrial processes and buildings may shift from fossil fuels to electricity, changing both the volume and timing of demand.

Supply Is Only Half of the Equation

Electricity must be available at the right time, in the right location, through adequate transmission and local distribution, with reliability resources available when conditions change.

The infrastructure challenge

The System Behind the Outlet Also Has a Cost

Producing electricity is only one part of the complete system.

Transmission

Moving electricity over long distances.

Distribution

Delivering power through substations, feeders, transformers, poles, and wires.

Storage

Shifting electricity across time and helping balance supply and demand.

Reliability

Maintaining service during heat, outages, equipment failures, and high-demand periods.

Vegetation Management

Tree trimming, hazard removal, inspection, planning, and clearance work around infrastructure.

Wildfire Protection

Hardening, monitoring, rebuilding, insurance, emergency work, and selected undergrounding.

The human consequence

The Cost of Electricity Affects More Than Electricity

At some point, rising energy costs begin competing with other uses of income.

Retirement and Fixed Income

Recurring energy costs can take a larger share of carefully planned income, especially when cooling, medical, or accessibility needs limit conservation.

Working and Young Families

Utility costs compete with childcare, transportation, groceries, education, savings, emergency reserves, and family experiences.

Homeowners and Renters

Electricity costs affect comfort, affordability, housing choices, and the ability to plan with confidence.

Small Businesses

Electricity can influence operating costs, pricing, competitiveness, hiring, and investment.

The Conservation Paradox

For years, many Californians conserved energy because it felt environmentally responsible.

A different possibility may be emerging. Some households may eventually conserve electricity primarily because they feel financially pressured to do so.

Energy efficiency is empowering when it is a choice. It feels very different when it becomes a necessity.

Could energy costs contribute to people leaving California?

Electricity alone does not determine where someone lives. But it joins housing, insurance, transportation, food, taxes, and other costs that collectively shape whether a household feels it can remain financially stable.

This page does not predict that people will leave. It recognizes that recurring energy costs can contribute to broader affordability pressure.

Why I created this page

This Comes From Care, Not Fear

I am not sharing this information to frighten anyone.

I am sharing it because energy decisions have long-term consequences, and too many people are expected to make those decisions without seeing the larger picture.

A utility bill is not just another monthly expense. It affects the comfort of our homes, our transportation, our savings, our retirement plans, our businesses, and the choices available to our families.

I believe people deserve access to understandable information before economic pressure forces them to act.

We may not control every policy, utility decision, wildfire cost, infrastructure project, or market change. But we can help one another become better informed.

The goal is awareness early enough that people still have choices.

The Informed Homeowner Movement

Awareness Should Be Shared, Not Sold

The Informed Homeowner Movement is a simple idea: Californians should help one another understand the costs, risks, contracts, assumptions, and long-term choices connected to home energy.

01

Better Information

Use verifiable sources, explain measurements, separate history from projections, and disclose limitations.

02

Better Questions

Ask what a number includes, who produced it, how it was calculated, and whether it matches the complete household experience.

03

Better Decisions

Respect that every household is different. Information should expand thoughtful choices, not pressure everyone into one answer.

What the movement stands for
  • Independent thinking rather than blind trust.
  • Plain-language explanations rather than technical fog.
  • Complete cost rather than one attractive number.
  • Questioning both utility-industry and energy-industry claims.
  • Sharing useful information with family, neighbors, and the community.
What the movement does not stand for
  • Fear-based predictions.
  • Utility bashing or political blame.
  • Assuming every forecast will happen exactly as modeled.
  • Telling every person to make the same decision.
  • Selecting facts only because they support a preferred conclusion.

Help one more person become informed

Someone You Care About May Not Know This

If this page helped you see California energy costs differently, share it with a family member, neighbor, retiree, working household, renter, homeowner, or small-business owner who may benefit from understanding it.

Not to alarm them. Not to sell them something. To help them ask better questions before the choices become more difficult.

Chart and source standards

How the Final Charts Will Be Added

This version intentionally uses placeholders so the page can be completed without publishing unresolved numbers.

Minimum requirements for every final chart
  • Clear title and time period.
  • Plain-language units and years without decimals.
  • Distinct colors, symbols, direct labels, and accessible alternative text.
  • Visible distinction between historical, preliminary, and forecast values.
  • Source, date, methodology, and important limitations.

Editorial standard: The finished page should never choose the number that creates the strongest reaction. It should use the measurement that best answers the stated question and clearly explain what that measurement does and does not represent.

The closing thought

The Goal Is Not Fear. The Goal Is Foresight.

No chart can tell anyone exactly what electricity will cost in 10 or 20 years. But history can show what has happened, public planning can show what is being prepared for, and people can decide whether continuing without an energy-cost plan is a risk they are comfortable accepting.

The question is no longer only:
“How much is my electric bill today?”

The larger question may be:
“How will electricity affect my household finances over the next 20 years?”

Better information. Better questions. Better decisions.
