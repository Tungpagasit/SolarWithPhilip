1Sunlight, roof direction and production

Solar only produces electricity while sunlight reaches the panels. Production rises after sunrise, is usually strongest near midday and falls toward sunset; at night production is zero. Shade, roof pitch, usable area and roof condition affect output. South-facing roof planes often provide strong annual exposure; east-facing planes favor morning production; west-facing planes favor afternoon production and can be strategically useful when evening electricity is expensive.

2Follow the electricity through the home

The inverter converts panel output into electricity the home can use. Active household loads use available solar first. When home demand exceeds current solar output, the difference comes from available battery energy or the grid. When current production exceeds home demand, the extra energy can charge the battery and, after home and battery needs are met, may be exported.

3Charge the battery before expecting it to serve the night

The array must be large enough to serve daytime loads and still leave surplus to charge the battery. An undersized array or heavy daytime load may prevent a full charge. At night, the battery can provide only what was stored, less its operating reserve and conversion losses. A self-consumption design may reserve roughly 10%–20% for backup, but the actual reserve should match the homeowner’s outage priorities and approved equipment settings. Backup power also depends on the circuits selected and battery output, not battery capacity alone.

4Understand imports, exports, billing and True-Up

The meter records electricity imported from and exported to the grid, while solar used directly inside the home may never pass through the utility meter. Under legacy NEM, annual charges and credits are generally reconciled at True-Up. If household usage rises while solar production does not, the remaining grid balance can rise. Under Net Billing, export credits and billing treatment differ, so the actual PG&E tariff and statement control.

5Set realistic grid and grandfathering expectations

A typical solar-and-battery system remains grid-connected. It does not automatically eliminate connection-related charges, grid purchases or a settlement that can be a charge or credit. Interconnection establishes the applicable tariff treatment, but grandfathering period, eligibility and conditions depend on the governing tariff. The key expectation is reduced grid dependence, not a claim that the utility disappears.

NEM 3 / Net Billing: what changed, why, and how design responds

When: The CPUC adopted the new tariff on December 15, 2022. It applies to new applicable PG&E, SCE and SDG&E interconnection applications beginning April 15, 2023.

Why: The tariff was designed to value exports according to grid value, encourage battery storage, support evening reliability and address costs allocated to customers without solar.

What changed: Solar used onsite avoids an import. Excess electricity exported to the grid is credited according to avoided-cost value rather than being treated like the full retail electricity imported later.

The PG&E problem: A home may export lower-value energy in the middle of the day and later buy higher-priced electricity after solar production falls.

How the design responds: Use solar directly, produce enough appropriate surplus to charge storage, and discharge the battery when the home would otherwise import expensive grid electricity. Do not let the low export credit become the whole story. The larger objective is reducing high-cost imports.

What must be designed correctly: Roof exposure, hourly usage, expected EV/HVAC/ADU loads, battery capacity and power, reserve setting, backup circuits, rate plan and remaining utility charges.

Why solar + battery is the practical next step

Compared with residential wind and ground-source systems, rooftop solar is broadly adaptable to many California homes and directly produces household electricity where it is used. Solar needs no delivered fuel, creates no operational air pollution at the home, uses distributed roof space and has no moving parts in the rooftop array.

When designed around the homeowner’s roof, hourly consumption, future loads, storage behavior and backup goals, solar plus battery can serve daytime loads, shift energy into evening hours, reduce costly grid imports and provide selected resilience. It will not make every utility charge disappear, and it must be priced and structured correctly.

Now that the energy system makes sense, the next question is which financial and responsibility structure best matches what the homeowner wants solved.
